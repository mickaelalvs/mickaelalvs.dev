<br />

Building your own devtools isn't some exotic idea. [Kent C. Dodds was already writing about it years ago](https://kentcdodds.com/blog/make-your-own-dev-tools): feature toggles, form autofill, switching backend environments on the fly. Back then, everything was hand-rolled: dynamic imports, custom tabs bolted onto the browser devtools, local files gitignored so you'd never commit them by mistake. All of it, hand-rolled.

We've known for a long time that your own devtools are a DX multiplier, but building them has always been tedious, from panel positioning to settings persistence. And more and more tools ship with their own: TanStack's (Query, Router, Form...), React Hook Form, or the Vue and Nuxt devtools. The problem is that each one lives in its own corner: a browser extension here (React DevTools, Redux DevTools), an in-app panel there, with zero coordination between them. No way to add a panel for your own specific needs. No way to make them talk to each other.

Meanwhile, on our own projects, we were piling up devtools without really noticing. Our homemade console for driving feature flags. The Redux DevTools panel. React DevTools. And every one that ships with the libraries we use.

Each of these tools, taken on its own, improves DX. Stacked on top of each other with no coordination, they do the opposite. **You can't see your screen anymore**, and you spend more time closing panels than debugging.

## 🔄 The pivot: TanStack Devtools

<br />

That's exactly the problem [TanStack Devtools](https://tanstack.com/devtools/latest) goes after. Its docs sum up the intent in one line: _"Build the inspector, not another debugging shell."_

It's not a pack that just bundles TanStack's own devtools together. It's the opposite: a framework-agnostic, extensible shell. Any library, or any team, can plug its own panel into it. Query, Router, Form, accessibility: those are just the first plugins installed in that shared shell.

The architecture breaks down into three layers:

- **The framework adapters** (`@tanstack/react-devtools`, `@tanstack/vue-devtools`, `@tanstack/solid-devtools`...): thin layers that expose the API in your framework.
- **The core shell** (`@tanstack/devtools`): written in Solid.js, regardless of your app's framework. It handles the plugin registry, tabs, settings, keyboard shortcuts.
- **The event system** (`@tanstack/devtools-event-client`): a typed pub/sub that connects your app to the Vite server over WebSocket or SSE.

And the shell isn't stuck with 2-3 tabs side by side. The _plugin workspace_ arranges several plugins at once into resizable panels, stackable or grouped into tabs, mini-IDE style.

At the scale of a codebase with several teams each plugging in their own devtools, this changes everything: no need to choose between Query, Router, and your custom panels, they coexist.

And the [multi-framework support](https://tanstack.com/devtools/latest/docs/framework) isn't just a checkbox in the docs. React, Vue, Solid, Svelte, Angular, Preact, or even vanilla JS with no framework at all: the stack doesn't matter. **A devtools plugin is nothing more than a component**: yours, in your framework, with your own conventions. A React team plugs in a React component, a Vue team a Vue component, each in its own language, inside the same shared shell.

In practice, for example, installing Query's official devtools looks like this:

```tsx
import {TanStackDevtools} from '@tanstack/react-devtools';
import {ReactQueryDevtoolsPanel} from '@tanstack/react-query-devtools';

<TanStackDevtools
  plugins={[
    {
      name: 'TanStack Query',
      render: <ReactQueryDevtoolsPanel />,
    },
  ]}
/>;
```

<Callout type="info">
  **Zero-cost in production by default.** `removeDevtoolsOnBuild` defaults to `true`: devtools don't even end up in the
  production bundle. Nothing to enable, nothing to do.
</Callout>

One honest caveat: the package is still **in alpha**, the API can shift. Nothing stops you from trying it, but keep that in mind before betting everything on it in production.

But even for an alpha package, digging into the [page detailing this architecture](https://tanstack.com/devtools/latest/docs/architecture), one thing struck me: every choice serves the same three goals. **Performance**: zero-cost in prod, a shell that never reimplements anything twice. On the **agnosticism** side, a neutral Solid.js core stays at the center while your plugins live in your framework. And for **customization**, there's the event system, the plugin workspace, the marketplace. Nothing is there by accident. There's easily enough here for several articles just on this architecture. Another time, maybe.

## 🧰 Building your team's devtools

<br />

I work on a Platform Frontend team, and part of our job is exactly this: taking care of DX. My goal, at its core, fits in one sentence: get teams as well-equipped as possible, with a smooth development experience and as little day-to-day friction as possible. On a large codebase, with multiple apps, shared packages, a design system, and migrations that keep coming back, putting the right tools in developers' hands isn't a nice-to-have. It's a necessity: every bit of friction you remove gets multiplied by the number of apps and developers who hit it.

We started like everyone else, with the official devtools: Query, Redux, and custom plugins. Then we wanted panels for our own needs:

- an analytics events log
- shortcuts to drive feature flipping
- a display of the current layout
- inspection of our state

Up until now, we built all the plumbing by hand for our custom stuff. And for the rest, official or not, it was either in an extension or in the browser console. On a codebase with multiple apps and teams, that cost isn't paid once: it's paid for every new need, in every app.

With TanStack Devtools, that work gets solved once, for everyone. A custom plugin, in its simplest form, is just a component you register through the `plugins` prop:

```tsx
<TanStackDevtools
  plugins={[
    {
      name: 'Feature Flipping',
      render: <FeatureFlippingPanel />,
    },
  ]}
/>
```

No class to extend, no API imposed by TanStack Devtools. `FeatureFlippingPanel` is a plain React component. The only real question left: how do you exchange data between this component and your application?

### With an existing provider or store

No plumbing needed: if a state manager already runs in your app (Redux, Zustand, Jotai...), a panel can subscribe to it like just another consumer. If `<TanStackDevtools>` is mounted inside one of your `<Provider>`s, the panel can talk to it exactly the way your application already does. Another option, just as direct, with no store at all: drive a flag from a URL query param, handy for feature flipping you can share via a link.

It works, and it's even the simplest path: the data already exists for the app itself, the panel just subscribes to it like another consumer, nothing to break. But it has a hard limit. A shared store only works within the same tab, the same process. No way to communicate between two tabs open at the same time, or between the browser and the terminal. For a cross-tab tracking need, an analytics log or debugging a Backend For Frontend (BFF) for instance, that's exactly where you hit a wall.

### With an event system

The event bus breaks down boundaries a store can't cross: between tabs, and even between the browser and the dev server. We'll see a concrete example further down where this changes everything.

It's also the only stable contract for published code. A third-party library, or a plugin published for others to use, can't know in advance what shape the store of an app installing it will have. It can only promise named, typed events. An external npm package has no way to know the exact shape of the store in the app that installs it.

<Callout type="note">
  Unlike a store, which exposes data that's already there for the app itself, the event bus requires an active step:
  adding an `emit()` at every point in your application code where you want visibility. The cost stays low, one line per
  point of interest, but it's not free the way tapping into an existing store is.
</Callout>

The official [_Building Custom Plugins_](https://tanstack.com/devtools/latest/docs/building-custom-plugins) guide sums up this plumbing in five steps:

- define an event map
- extend `EventClient`
- `emit()` from the app code
- listen with `on()` in the panel
- register everything

Simplified, it looks roughly like this:

```ts
import {EventClient} from '@tanstack/devtools-event-client';

type MyEvents = {
  'feature-flag-toggle': {flag: string; enabled: boolean};
};

class TeamEventClient extends EventClient<MyEvents> {
  constructor() {
    super({pluginId: 'team-devtools'});
  }
}

export const teamEventClient = new TeamEventClient();
```

For feature flipping, the panel is in control: you toggle it in the devtool, it `emit()`s.

```tsx
function FeatureFlippingPanel() {
  const [flags, setFlags] = useState<Record<string, boolean>>({
    'new-checkout': false,
  });

  const toggle = (flag: string) => {
    const enabled = !flags[flag];
    setFlags((prev) => ({...prev, [flag]: enabled}));
    teamEventClient.emit('feature-flag-toggle', {flag, enabled});
  };

  return (
    <ul>
      {Object.entries(flags).map(([flag, enabled]) => (
        <li key={flag}>
          <button onClick={() => toggle(flag)}>
            {flag}: {enabled ? '✅' : '❌'}
          </button>
        </li>
      ))}
    </ul>
  );
}
```

And on the app side, locally, a small module listens with `on()` to override real values with the devtool's:

```ts
const overrides = {};

teamEventClient.on('feature-flag-toggle', (e) => {
  overrides[e.payload.flag] = e.payload.enabled;
});

function isEnabled(flag) {
  return overrides[flag] ?? realValue(flag);
}
```

<FeatureFlagPanelDemo />

The question to ask: same-tab, with a store already there? Just tap into it directly. Cross-tab, or outside the browser? That's the event bus's job.

Either way, the panel stays that same plain component from earlier. What changes is just where the data comes from.

**The real DX win isn't any particular panel.** It's that every feature team now only has to define its events and its component. The panel problem, positioning, shortcuts, persistence: solved once and for all, upstream.

One thing to watch, though: this code is still dev code. Without discipline, it can pollute your components or end up in the production bundle for nothing. `@tanstack/devtools-utils` offers factories for exactly this (`createReactPlugin`, `createVuePlugin`...) that return a `[Plugin, NoOpPlugin]` pair, so keeping a panel tree-shakable doesn't take extra thought every time:

```tsx
const [FeatureFlippingPlugin, NoOpPlugin] = createReactPlugin({
  name: 'Feature Flipping',
  Component: FeatureFlippingPanel,
});

const ActivePlugin = process.env.NODE_ENV === 'development' ? FeatureFlippingPlugin : NoOpPlugin;
```

## 🎯 Go to source: the click that changes everything

<br />

If you go through the Vite plugin, it's not limited to a single feature, it comes with [a whole list of built-in features](https://tanstack.com/devtools/latest/docs/vite-plugin#features): console piping, a plugin marketplace, automatic stripping in prod... But if there's one that sums up the tool's DX philosophy, it's go to source.

At build time, the Vite plugin (`@tanstack/devtools-vite`) parses your JSX/TSX with `oxc-parser` and injects a `data-tsd-source="file:line:column"` attribute on every element. In dev, you hold down a simple, configurable shortcut to hover any element on the page. Not just inside the devtools panel: the whole page becomes inspectable. One click, and the editor opens straight to the right file, at the right line, via [`launch-editor`](https://github.com/vitejs/launch-editor): the same library Vite uses internally. It detects your running editor on its own and works with a wide range of editors (VS Code, the JetBrains suite, Sublime Text, Vim...) with zero configuration.

<Callout type="note">
  This only works with Vite for now: no Webpack, Turbopack, or Rspack support mentioned in the docs.
</Callout>

This maps directly onto the architecture described earlier, plus one extra piece. The Vite plugin adds a _build-time_ layer, upstream of the three runtime layers: it's the one injecting `data-tsd-source` before the app even runs. The hotkey and the hover highlight come from the core shell. And layer 3, the event system, is the pipe that carries the click's info to the right place. The browser emits, through that same `EventClient`/event bus mechanism, a request to the Vite server. The server receives it and calls `launch-editor` to open the file at the right line. Exactly the point made earlier: this is a concrete case where the data has to cross the browser → Node process boundary, something a shared store can't do.

<Callout type="info">
  React DevTools offers something similar, but it stays tied to React and to the browser extension. TanStack's go to
  source works for any plugin, because it relies on a build-time injection, not runtime introspection of one specific
  framework.
</Callout>

<GoToSourceDemo />

It's a feature I loved the moment I tried it. On large applications, on a complex design system, hunting down by hand the file responsible for a piece of UI can quickly turn into a treasure hunt. Here, one click is enough.

It's easy to underestimate a detail like this. But on a large codebase, for onboarding new devs, it's huge: no more grepping a CSS class name or a piece of text to find the component behind a visual bug. You click, you're in the file.

The same principle applies to logs. With the Vite plugin, every browser `console.log` shows a clickable `file:line`, and logs flow both ways: what shows up in the browser console also surfaces in your terminal, and the reverse for server/SSR logs. No more hunting for which terminal or which tab a log landed in.

## 🧩 Going further: more custom devtools to build

<br />

Now that you know how it works, ideas will come on their own, as you go about your daily work. [The plugin marketplace](https://tanstack.com/devtools/latest/docs/third-party-plugins) isn't even just a nice idea: it already exists.

- **Accessibility overlay**: `@tanstack/devtools-a11y` is a good example, built on `axe-core`, with a visual overlay, click-to-source navigation to the offending element, and a JSON or CSV report export.

Nothing stops a platform team from publishing its own internal plugin on the same principle. And for the rest, I'm sure you won't run out of ideas: feature flag toggles, network override / mock switcher, time-travel debugging... The list of official plugins is growing fast too: Query, Router, and Form each have theirs, so does Pacer (animations/transitions), and there's even a dedicated TanStack AI plugin for inspecting messages, tokens, and streaming tool calls. The shell didn't stay confined to data-fetching.

## 🚀 What actually changes

<br />

Before, this piece of DX was built by hand, or worse, not built at all: you lived with the friction, the pointless back-and-forth, a longer onboarding, telling yourself it was the price to pay. TanStack Devtools makes it trivial: install a shell, define an event client, plug in a panel.

The real DX promise isn't having a nice panel to inspect Query's cache. It's giving every team the infrastructure to build its own devtools, without reinventing the wheel every time, and without ending up, one day, with five floating panels hiding your own screen.

Take care of your tools: a lack of DX never shows, until the day you fix it. Your teams deserve better, try TanStack's.
